Questions

The ones worth answering before you install it, and the two that are genuinely awkward.

What leaves your Mac

What gets uploaded?

Nothing from a meeting. Not the audio, not the transcript, not your notes, not the summary. Recording, transcription, speaker identification and the write-up all run on models on your Mac, and once the one-time speech-model download below has happened, the whole thing works in airplane mode.

The app makes exactly two kinds of network request, and both are about the app, not about you. It checks its GitHub Releases feed once a day for a newer version, and downloads that version if you say yes. No account, no analytics, no system profile attached to either one. A check never starts while you're recording, and you can turn the whole thing off in Settings → Updates. Separately, macOS fetches Apple's speech model for your language the first time you record (once per language, and it's the OS doing it, not Cheerio).

Can my own AI agents read my meetings?

Yes, two ways, both local. Point Settings → Callback at any command and Cheerio runs it when a meeting finishes processing. The command gets the whole meeting as JSON on stdin and at the path in CHEERIO_EXPORT_PATH, with the meeting's ID, kind and title in the environment. Nothing from the transcript ever lands on the command line.

There's also a small MCP server inside the app bundle, so an MCP client (Claude Code, Claude Desktop, Codex, anything that speaks the protocol) can look things up on demand. Five tools, all read-only: list meetings, search them, fetch one in full, fetch just the transcript, fetch the action items. It can't write, delete, start a recording or run a command. Your client launches it over a pipe. No networking path at all. The setup snippets are in Settings → Agents.

Honestly, neither one is a feature so much as an admission. The file was already sitting on your Mac, and anything that can read a file could always read your meetings.

Isn't “nothing leaves your machine” at odds with “your agents can read this”?

Fair question, and here's the seam. Cheerio never sends a transcript anywhere. If the command you name in the callback is an agent running locally, nothing changes hands and the guarantee holds end to end. If it's an agent backed by a cloud model, that agent forwards whatever you handed it. Usually that's the whole reason you pointed it there. But it's your decision, made in a text field, and it's the exact moment the local guarantee stops applying.

Cheerio's promise is about what Cheerio does. It doesn't quietly extend to a choice you make in Settings, and it would be dishonest to write it like it did.

Do I have to tell people I'm recording?

We can't answer that for you, and nothing here is legal advice. Recording law differs by country and by state, it depends on who's in the conversation and where each of them is, and some places require everyone's consent, not just yours.

What we can tell you is what the software does, which is usually what people are actually asking. Cheerio doesn't announce itself. A notetaker that joins a call shows up in the participant list where everybody can see it. Cheerio records the audio your Mac is already playing and the mic you're already using, so nothing gets sent and nothing shows up on anyone else's screen. It also doesn't publish or upload anything. The recording, the transcript and the notes stay on your Mac.

Worth being clear-eyed about two things, though. That bot in the call was doing a disclosure job, and some people were relying on it without ever deciding to. With no bot, telling people yourself is the only thing that tells them. And “nobody else can see this” is not the same statement as “nobody needed to be asked.” Only one of those is about the software.

If you'd say “mind if I record this?” before starting a hosted notetaker, nothing about Cheerio changes that.

What it needs

What do I need to run it?

macOS 26 (Tahoe) or later, on an Apple Silicon Mac. There's no Intel build and there won't be one (the speaker model runs on the Neural Engine). Cheerio asks for microphone and system-audio recording permission; calendar access is optional and only affects meeting titles. Summaries and action items come from Apple's on-device language model, so you need Apple Intelligence-capable hardware with it switched on. Without it you still get the full transcript, just no write-up. On disk, the thing that grows is the audio, and that's what the retention setting bounds (seven days of it by default). Transcripts, notes and summaries are small text and stay until you delete the meeting.

The version requirement isn't us being cautious. Transcription and summarizing use frameworks that arrived in macOS 26, and process taps on system audio are a recent Core Audio facility. On an older macOS there's nothing to build with.

Does it work with Zoom, Meet, Teams, or a meeting in a room?

The capture side doesn't care, because it doesn't integrate with any of them. Cheerio records your mic and everything coming out of your Mac as two separate streams. Anything that makes sound gets captured, and nothing needs to support Cheerio first (including a phone on speaker, or a conversation across a desk with no software involved at all).

The honest caveat: in-person recording is verified end to end, and a live video call hasn't been through the same check yet. It captures the audio. The full path just hasn't been confirmed on a real Zoom or Meet call.

How many people can it tell apart?

Four voices per audio channel. That's a hard limit of the speaker model, not a setting. A call with three people on the other end plus you is comfortable. A room of eight isn't; the extra voices get merged into slots that are already taken. The app can warn you about the roster (pick more than four enrolled voices for one meeting and it names which ones it couldn't check for), but a voice it was never told about just merges, silently. The model can't report someone it doesn't know exists.

Names only come back for voices you've enrolled, and samples want at least thirty seconds. Shorter ones measurably cause one person to get split across two speakers. Any label you fix by hand outranks the model and stays fixed.

Why isn't it on the Mac App Store?

Because it can't be. Capturing system audio needs the App Sandbox turned off, and the App Store requires it on. That isn't a preference or a shortcut. A sandboxed build creates the audio tap successfully at every step, reports no error, prompts for nothing, and then records pure digital silence. We measured it on identical code: the sandboxed build peaked at 0.0, the unsandboxed one at −1.8 dBFS.

It comes from this site instead. Builds are Developer ID-signed and notarized by Apple, so they open without warnings or right-click ceremony, and the app updates itself after that.

Getting things out

Where can I send a finished meeting?

Three routes, all yours to control. Export any meeting as Markdown and put the file wherever you keep things. Point the callback at a command and Cheerio hands the meeting to it as JSON the moment it's ready. Or let an MCP client read the library directly, on demand.

What doesn't exist yet: automatic export into a folder (an Obsidian vault, say) or any send-to-service integration. The callback is the general form of both, and it's a command line, so a two-line script gets you the folder version today.

What happens if I stop using it?

You drag the app to the trash. No account to close, no subscription to cancel, nobody to email about deleting a copy on a server, because Cheerio never made one. (The one exception is the one you configured: anything a callback handed to a cloud-backed agent lives by that service's rules, not Cheerio's. Same seam as the agents question above.) Your meetings live in one folder, ~/Library/Application Support/app.cheerio.mac. Delete it and they're gone. Back it up and they're safe. Take anything you want out as Markdown first; it's plain text that every other tool can already read.

That's the whole exit. It's also why there's no free trial, no onboarding funnel and no reactivation email. None of the machinery that would make leaving hard ever got built.

The project

Why is it free? What's the catch?

Cheerio is built by Obvios, a consultancy that works alongside companies learning to get real use out of AI. Building the small missing tools is part of that work, and Cheerio is one of them. We made it because the alternative was a subscription and an upload. It isn't a product line, it isn't funded by users, and it doesn't need to earn anything to keep existing.

The catch, such as it is, is the honest one: it's a single-user Mac app maintained by a small team, not a company with a support department. In exchange there's no account, no telemetry, no analytics, no crash reporting, and nothing to sell (including you, since your meetings never reach anybody who could).

If it's ever monetized, the constraint we've committed to is that monetization can't add a hoop. No sign-in for a paid tier, no sync that becomes the default path, no team feature that needs an account, no telemetry you can't switch off, no format only this app can read.

Why “Cheerio”?

It's a breakfast joke, a goodbye, and a ring, in that order. The full confession is at the bottom of the homepage.

Can I see the source, or fix something?

All of it is on GitHub under the MIT license, including the build instructions. Bugs and suggestions go in the issues.

Still deciding?

It's free, it's about a hundred megabytes, and it takes a walkthrough and a thirty-second voice sample to be useful. The fastest way to answer the rest of your questions is to have it running before your next meeting.
