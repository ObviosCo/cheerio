Meeting notes that never leave your Mac.

Cheerio records both sides of a meeting, transcribes them on-device, tells the participants apart by name, and merges your rough notes with the transcript into something structured. No account, no subscription, and no bot joining the call.

Get CheerioStar on GitHub

Free and MIT-licensed. Requires macOS 26 or later on Apple Silicon.

What it does

Six things, and it does them locally.

  • Captures without a bot

    Two independent streams — your microphone, and everyone else through a Core Audio process tap on system output. Works with Zoom, Meet, Teams, or anything else that makes sound, because it records audio rather than joining a call. Nothing has to support it first.

  • Transcribes on the machine

    Each stream gets its own transcriber, which is where the Me and Them split comes from. The live transcript appears as you talk; the final text is kept with timestamps.

  • Tells people apart by name

    The channel split can't separate three people sitting in one room, so a diarization pass runs over the recording afterwards. Enroll a voice once and it comes back named instead of Speaker 2. Fix a label by hand and your correction outranks the model.

  • Treats your rough notes as the point

    A scratchpad sits beside the live transcript. What you bothered to type is the strongest signal about what mattered, so it's the first input to the summary, not an afterthought.

  • Keeps the audio only as long as you want

    Raw audio is written per channel so a failed transcription isn't fatal, then purged on your schedule — immediately, 24 hours, 7 days, 30 days, or never. Seven days is the default.

  • Searches and exports

    Browse past meetings, search across titles, notes, transcripts and speaker names, and export any meeting as Markdown.

Known issue · use headphones

With meeting audio playing out loud, your microphone hears your speakers, so remote participants land in both channels and the transcript duplicates itself — which then skews the summary. Headphones avoid it entirely. Echo cancellation on the mic input is the next thing being built.

Names arrive after you stop

Speaker identification runs over the recorded audio once the meeting ends, so during a recording the transcript only knows which of the two audio channels a line came from. In an in-person meeting everyone is on the microphone, which makes the live transcript look like it can't tell anyone apart. It can, a moment later.

The full status list is in the README, including the four-speaker cap and what isn't built yet.

Why it runs on your machine

Every model Cheerio uses is already on your Mac — Apple's for speech and summarising, plus one bundled model for telling voices apart. Recording a meeting, transcribing it and writing it up need no network at all. The app makes exactly two kinds of request, both about itself: it checks its GitHub Releases feed once a day for a new version, and downloads one if you say yes. Those are ordinary HTTPS fetches — no account, no analytics, no system profile attached — and you can switch the checks off. The other asterisk is macOS itself, fetching the speech model for your language the first time you record.

That's a consequence of how it's built rather than a promise being sold to you. It does mean the app works on a plane, doesn't slow down when someone else's service does, and doesn't change behaviour the week a hosted model gets swapped underneath it.
